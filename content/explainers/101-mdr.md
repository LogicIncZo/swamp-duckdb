# MDR 101: Understanding India's Merchant Discount Rate

## What is MDR?

**Merchant Discount Rate (MDR)** is the fee a merchant pays to their acquiring bank and payment service providers for accepting digital payments from customers. It is expressed as a percentage of the transaction value and is deducted from the amount settled to the merchant. [^1]

When a customer swipes a card at a shop, scans a UPI QR code, or pays online, several entities work behind the scenes to process that transaction — the acquiring bank (merchant's bank), the issuing bank (customer's bank), the payment network (RuPay, Visa, Mastercard), and often a payment aggregator or gateway. MDR is the primary revenue mechanism that funds this entire chain. [^2]

### How MDR Differs from Interchange

MDR is the total fee charged to the merchant. It is typically split into three components: [^3]

| Component | Recipient | Description |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Interchange Fee** | Issuing bank (customer's bank) | Fee paid by the acquirer to the issuer for processing the transaction. Set by the card network (Visa/Mastercard/RuPay). |
| **Network/Assessment Fee** | Payment network (Visa/Mastercard/NPCI) | Fee for using the network's infrastructure. |
| **Acquirer Markup** | Acquiring bank / Payment aggregator | The acquirer's margin for onboarding the merchant, providing PoS terminals, and managing settlement. |

Interchange is the largest component — typically 70-80% of the total MDR. [^4] For a typical credit card transaction with 2% MDR, roughly ₹1.40-1.60 of every ₹100 goes to the issuing bank, ₹0.10-0.18 to the card network, and the remainder to the acquirer/processor.

### Key Milestones in MDR Regulation

- **December 2017**: RBI caps MDR for debit cards — 0.25% for transactions ≤₹1,000, 0.5% for ₹1,000-2,000, 1% for >₹2,000 [^5]
- **December 2017**: Government notifies that MDR on government transactions will be borne by the government [^6]
- **January 2018**: RBI rationalises MDR into a two-tier structure based on merchant turnover (≤₹20 lakh vs >₹20 lakh) [^7]
- **January 2020**: Government mandates zero MDR on UPI and RuPay debit card transactions via Section 10A of the PSS Act [^8]
- **December 2021**: Government launches incentive scheme for low-value BHIM-UPI P2M transactions (subsidy at 0.15% for transactions ≤₹2,000) [^9]
- **August 2022**: RBI releases Discussion Paper on Charges in Payment Systems — flags credit card MDR for the first time [^10]
- **March 2023**: NPCI introduces 1.1% interchange on PPI-based UPI transactions >₹2,000 [^11]
- **March 2025**: Payments Council of India writes to PM Modi requesting rollback of zero-MDR [^12]
- **March 2026**: Parliamentary Standing Committee on Finance recommends return of MDR for large UPI merchants [^13]
- **August 2026**: Taxation and Other Laws (Amendment) Bill, 2026 amends Section 10A of the PSS Act, opening the door for targeted MDR on UPI [^14]

## How MDR Works

### The Transaction Flow

1. **Customer initiates payment** via card swipe, QR scan, or online checkout.
2. **Acquiring bank** routes the transaction through the payment network to the issuing bank.
3. **Issuing bank** verifies funds and authorises the transaction.
4. **Payment network** settles the transaction between banks.
5. **Acquiring bank** deducts the MDR from the transaction amount and credits the net amount to the merchant's account.

For example, on a ₹1,000 credit card payment with 2% MDR, the merchant receives ₹980. The acquiring bank keeps its margin, pays the interchange to the issuing bank, and the network fee to the card scheme.

### Who Actually Bears the Cost?

While MDR is formally charged to the merchant, the economic incidence often falls on the consumer through:

- **Higher prices** — merchants may embed the cost into product pricing
- **Surcharging** — some merchants illegally add a fee for card/UPI payments (see Consumer Rights section)
- **Discounts for cash** — merchants offer lower prices for cash payments, effectively passing the MDR cost to digital payment users

As RBI Governor Sanjay Malhotra observed in August 2026: "Ultimately it is the consumer in some way or the other who's paying... it may not be the same consumer. It may be the general economy." [^15]

## Types/Categories of MDR in India

### By Payment Instrument

**1. Debit Card MDR (Non-RuPay)**

RBI has prescribed MDR caps based on merchant size and acceptance mode: [^7]

| Merchant Category | Physical POS / Online | QR Code-based |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Small (turnover ≤₹20 lakh/year) | ≤0.40% (cap ₹200/txn) | ≤0.30% (cap ₹200/txn) |
| Large (turnover >₹20 lakh/year) | ≤0.90% (cap ₹1,000/txn) | ≤0.80% (cap ₹1,000/txn) |
| Government transactions | Flat ₹5 (≤₹1,000), ₹10 (₹1,001-2,000), ≤0.50% (>₹2,000, cap ₹250) | Same as physical POS |

**2. RuPay Debit Card MDR — Zero**

Since January 1, 2020, no MDR is charged on RuPay debit card transactions. [^8]

**3. UPI MDR — Zero (with exceptions)**

Since January 1, 2020, no MDR is charged on UPI transactions. [^8] Exceptions:

- **PPI-on-UPI**: Since April 1, 2023, NPCI charges an interchange fee of up to 1.1% on UPI merchant transactions >₹2,000 when the payment originates from a Prepaid Payment Instrument (wallet) rather than a bank account. [^11]
- **RuPay Credit-on-UPI**: MDR applies as per credit card rates since this is a credit product.

**4. Credit Card MDR — Market-Determined**

RBI has **not** issued any regulatory mandate on MDR for credit card transactions. [^10] Market rates typically range from 1.5% to 3.0% depending on card type, merchant category, and transaction channel (online vs in-person). Premium cards (American Express, Diners Club, Visa Signature/Infinite) command the highest MDRs.

### By Acceptance Mode

| Mode | Typical MDR Range |
| --- | --- |
| Physical POS (card swipe/dip/tap) | 0.4-3.0% |
| QR code (BharatQR/UPI QR) | 0-0.8% |
| Online/E-commerce | 0.5-3.5% |
| Recurring/EMI | Varies by arrangement |

- **Payment Aggregators**: Razorpay, Paytm, BillDesk, CCAvenue — sit between merchants and banks, especially for e-commerce
- **Payment Gateways**: Subset of aggregators focused on online transaction routing
- **PSPs (Payment Service Providers)**: Google Pay, PhonePe, Paytm — UPI apps that facilitate P2M transactions

### Industry Advocacy Bodies

- **Payments Council of India (PCI)**: Industry body representing 180+ payment companies; has been the primary lobbyist for reintroducing MDR on UPI/RuPay [^12]

## Regulatory Framework

### Payment and Settlement Systems Act, 2007 (PSS Act)

The PSS Act is the foundational legislation governing payment systems in India. It designates RBI as the regulator and provides the legal framework for payment system operators. [^24]

**Section 10A** — the provision that mandated zero MDR — was originally tied to Section 269SU of the Income Tax Act, 1961, which required merchants to accept prescribed electronic payment modes. Together, these provisions made it illegal for banks and PSPs to levy MDR on UPI and RuPay debit card transactions. [^8]

**The 2026 Amendment**: The Taxation and Other Laws (Amendment) Bill, 2026, introduced in the Lok Sabha on August 4, 2026, amends Section 10A. It replaces the blanket prohibition with a framework under which the Central Government may, by gazette notification, designate which electronic payment modes are exempt from charges — and by omission, which are not. This means:

- The zero-MDR protection is no longer automatic or universal
- The government can carve out exceptions (e.g., large merchants, high-value transactions) without parliamentary debate
- The NPCI-headed "UPI and Services Steering Committee" will decide on specific MDR rates if and when introduced [^14]

### Government Incentive Scheme

To partially compensate banks and PSPs for the zero-MDR policy, the government launched the **Incentive Scheme for Promotion of RuPay Debit Cards and Low-Value BHIM-UPI (P2M) Transactions** in December 2021. Key features: [^9]

- Covers UPI P2M transactions up to ₹2,000 only
- Subsidy capped at 0.15% of transaction value (15 basis points)
- Shared among banks, PSPs, and third-party apps
- **Large merchants are not covered**

**Subsidy trend**:
- FY 2021-22: ₹1,389 crore [^25]
- FY 2024-25: ₹1,922.77 crore [^19]
- FY 2025-26: ₹2,196.21 crore [^19]
- FY 2026-27 (budgeted): ₹2,000 crore [^19]

The Parliamentary Standing Committee on Finance noted that cumulative subsidies of ₹8,730 crore from FY 2021-22 to FY 2024-25 covered only **11% of the industry's actual costs** and **14% of potential MDR revenue**. [^13]

### RBI's Role

RBI regulates MDR for debit cards (caps prescribed) and oversees the payments ecosystem. Key positions: [^10]

- **Debit card MDR**: Prescribed caps under Section 10(2) read with Section 18 of the PSS Act
- **Credit card MDR**: No regulatory mandate; RBI's August 2022 Discussion Paper flagged the absence of regulation for the first time, seeking feedback on whether credit card MDR should be capped, and whether interchange (rather than total MDR) should be regulated
- **UPI MDR**: Governed by Section 10A of the PSS Act (as amended)
- **No-surcharge rule**: RBI has mandated that merchants must not pass on debit card MDR charges to customers [^7]

### NPCI's PPI Interchange (2023)

In March 2023, NPCI introduced interchange fees of up to **1.1%** on UPI merchant transactions above ₹2,000 when the payment is made from a Prepaid Payment Instrument (PPI/wallet). [^11] This was controversial because:

- It effectively reintroduced fees on a subset of UPI transactions
- Some legal scholars (including Ashish Das, IIT Bombay) argued it contradicted Section 10A of the PSS Act, which prohibits charges on UPI [^26]
- Approximately 15-20% of merchants reportedly requested their banks to disable RuPay credit card payments on UPI due to MDR concerns [^27]

## Consumer Rights Analysis

### The Right to Pay Digitally Without Extra Charges

The consumer's most important right in the MDR context is this: **MDR is a cost between the merchant and their bank. It must not be passed on to the customer.**

For **debit card transactions**, RBI has explicitly directed that "banks are advised to ensure that merchants on-boarded by them do not pass on MDR charges to customers while accepting payments through debit cards." [^7] The Government of India reinforced this in a December 2017 gazette notification, requiring merchant agreements to include a no-surcharge clause. [^28]

For **credit card transactions**, the legal position is more nuanced. RBI's August 2022 Discussion Paper acknowledged that "RBI has not issued any regulatory mandate or intervened on MDR for credit card transactions." [^10] The same paper noted that some merchants recover credit card costs by levying surcharges or offering cash discounts — a practice that, while common, effectively penalises digital payment users.

### What This Means for Consumers in Practice

1. **Surcharging on debit cards/UPI is illegal.** If a merchant adds a fee for UPI or debit card payment, the consumer can file a complaint with RBI. [^29]
2. **Surcharging on credit cards exists in a grey area.** While card network rules generally prohibit surcharging, RBI has not issued a regulatory mandate for credit cards, and the practice persists. [^10]
3. **Cash discounts are legal but discriminatory.** A merchant offering a lower price for cash payments is effectively making digital payment users bear the MDR cost indirectly.
4. **Convenience fees on e-commerce are common.** Many platforms (IRCTC, airlines, event ticketing) add a "convenience fee" that functions like MDR. These are often disclosed but not always transparent about whether they reflect actual processing costs or include a markup.

### The 2026 Amendment — What Changes for Consumers

The amendment to Section 10A of the PSS Act is the most significant development in MDR policy since zero MDR was mandated. For consumers: [^14]

- **P2P UPI transactions remain protected** — the government has clarified these will not attract MDR
- **Low-value P2M transactions are expected to remain free** — MDR, if introduced, will be "threshold-based" (likely targeting transactions above ₹2,000 and/or merchants above a turnover threshold, e.g., ₹50 crore)
- **Proposed rates are nominal** — reports suggest 5-25 basis points (0.05-0.25%), far below debit card MDR (0.4-0.9%) and credit card MDR (1.5-3.0%)
- **The key risk is indirect pass-through** — even if merchants are formally charged, they may embed the cost in prices, affecting all consumers regardless of payment method
- **Executive power concern** — the amendment allows the government to change MDR exemptions by gazette notification without parliamentary debate, reducing democratic oversight

## Privacy Implications

MDR itself is a financial transaction — it does not directly involve personal data collection beyond what is already required to process the payment. However, there are indirect privacy implications:

### Data Generated by Payment Processing

Every MDR-bearing transaction generates data about: the merchant's business (what they sell, transaction volumes, peak times), the customer's spending patterns (where they shop, how much they spend), and the payment instrument used. This data flows through acquirers, networks, and PSPs. [^30]

### Zero MDR's Privacy Paradox

The zero-MDR policy accelerated UPI adoption to 241 billion transactions annually. [^16] This scale of data generation raises privacy concerns:

- **Transaction surveillance**: Every UPI transaction is logged by banks, PSPs, and NPCI. At 66 crore daily transactions, this is a comprehensive financial surveillance dataset
- **Data monetisation**: Payment data is valuable for credit scoring, targeted advertising, and merchant analytics. Zero MDR may have accelerated the creation of this data asset
- **Government access**: Under the PSS Act and related regulations, government agencies can access payment data for investigation and policy purposes

### MDR and Data as Implicit Compensation

One argument (made by scholars like Ashish Das) is that the real cost of zero MDR is not just the ₹20,700 crore in operating costs — it includes the **opportunity cost of data monetisation** that payment companies forgo by processing transactions they cannot directly monetise. [^26] If MDR is reintroduced, it may reduce the incentive for payment companies to monetise transaction data through other means — or it may simply add revenue on top of existing data monetisation.

## Safeguards

### For Consumers

1. **No-surcharge protection on debit cards/UPI**: RBI mandate prohibits merchants from passing MDR to customers on debit card transactions [^7]
2. **Complaint mechanism**: Consumers can file complaints with RBI (https://cms.rbi.org.in) if a merchant levies illegal surcharges
3. **Section 10A (as amended)**: Even after amendment, the government has stated that P2P transactions and low-value P2M transactions will remain protected [^14]
4. **Transparent pricing**: While not yet mandated, increasing regulatory attention on MDR may drive greater transparency in how payment costs are disclosed

### For Small Merchants

1. **Zero MDR continues** for UPI and RuPay debit cards as of August 2026 — no MDR has been implemented yet
2. **Government subsidy**: Transactions ≤₹2,000 qualify for the incentive scheme (0.15% subsidy) [^9]
3. **RBI's merchant categorisation**: Small merchants (turnover ≤₹20 lakh/year) already benefit from lower MDR caps on non-RuPay debit cards (0.30-0.40% vs 0.80-0.90%) [^7]
4. **Proposed MDR targets large merchants**: Any future MDR is expected to apply only to merchants above a significant turnover threshold (₹50 crore has been mentioned), protecting small merchants [^19]

### What Consumers Should Watch For

- **Nominal MDR creep**: Once the legal barrier is removed, rates could increase over time. The initial 5-25 bps could rise in future years
- **Broadening scope**: The threshold for MDR applicability could be lowered over time — from ₹50-crore merchants to smaller ones
- **Payment method steering**: Merchants might steer customers toward lower-MDR payment methods (or away from higher-MDR ones), limiting consumer choice
- **Invisible price increases**: Even if MDR is never charged to the consumer directly, the cumulative effect on prices across the economy is real

## Complaints & Grievance Redressal

### If a Merchant Charges You Extra for UPI or Debit Card Payment

1. **Document the incident**: Screenshot the receipt, note the merchant name, transaction amount, and extra charge
2. **Complain to the merchant's bank**: RBI has directed acquiring banks to ensure their merchants do not surcharge. The bank is obligated to act on such complaints
3. **File a complaint with RBI**: Use the RBI Integrated Ombudsman Scheme at [https://cms.rbi.org.in](https://cms.rbi.org.in). This is a single-window mechanism for lodging complaints against banks, NBFCs, and payment system operators
4. **Consumer protection forums**: You can also approach the District Consumer Disputes Redressal Commission under the Consumer Protection Act, 2019

### If a Payment App Deducts Charges from Your Account

UPI P2P transactions must not attract any charges. If a payment app deducts fees for a regular UPI transfer, this is a violation. Complaints can be filed with:

- The payment app's grievance officer (mandatory under RBI guidelines)
- RBI's Integrated Ombudsman Scheme
- NPCI (as the UPI network operator)

### Relevant Legal Provisions

- **Payment and Settlement Systems Act, 2007**: Section 10(2) read with Section 18 — empowers RBI to regulate charges [^24]
- **Section 10A (as amended)**: Framework for exempting payment modes from charges [^14]
- **Consumer Protection Act, 2019**: Unfair trade practices, including charging extra for digital payments
- **RBI Master Direction on KYC, 2022**: Merchant onboarding requirements that include no-surcharge clauses [^31]
- **Income Tax Act, Section 269SU**: Requires certain businesses to accept prescribed electronic payment modes

## The Debate: Should MDR Return?

### The Industry's Case

The payments industry — represented by the Payments Council of India and individual players — argues that: [^12][^20]

- Zero MDR is financially unsustainable at UPI's current scale (24,161 crore transactions/year)
- The ₹2,000 crore government subsidy covers only 11% of actual operating costs
- Continuous investment in cybersecurity, fraud prevention, and infrastructure requires sustainable revenue
- Large merchants already pay MDR on credit cards and non-RuPay debit cards; a nominal UPI MDR would not materially impact them
- ThePayments Council proposed 25 basis points (0.25%) for large merchants

### The Consumer's Case

From a consumer rights perspective, the counter-arguments are: [^13][^25]

- **MDR is a regressive cost**: It falls disproportionately on smaller merchants who have thinner margins, and is ultimately passed to consumers through higher prices
- **UPI is a public good**: Like roads and electricity, the argument goes, basic payment infrastructure should be funded through general taxation or industry investment, not per-transaction fees
- **The subsidy gap is a policy choice, not a fundamental problem**: The government could increase subsidies, impose a tech levy on payment companies, or fund NPCI directly
- **Slippery slope**: Once the legal barrier (Section 10A) is removed, there is no guarantee that MDR will remain limited to large merchants or high-value transactions
- **Data monetisation**: Payment companies already benefit from access to massive transaction datasets; MDR would be revenue on top of existing data advantages
- **Global precedent**: Brazil's Pix and the EU's regulated interchange show that real-time payments can function with low or zero merchant fees

### The Middle Ground

The government's current stated position attempts to balance these concerns: [^14]

- MDR, if introduced, would be **threshold-based** and **nominal**
- **P2P transactions** would remain free
- **Small merchants** would remain protected
- **The NPCI steering committee** would determine rates, not the government directly

The critical question for consumers is whether these protections will hold as UPI continues to grow and fiscal pressures on the subsidy mount.
